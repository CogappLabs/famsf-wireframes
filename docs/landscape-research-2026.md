# Collection Project 2026

## Landscape Research

**Owner:** Stephanie Amaro

# **Context**

The 2026 Collections Project is a strategic initiative to modernize how FAMSF's digital collection is managed and experienced online. This discovery process identifies industry standards, innovative UX patterns, and technical benchmarks to help elevate FAMSF’s digital presence.

---

# **Key learning goals**

**User Experience & Information Architecture**

* **Multiple persona:** How does the IA serve the Explorer (browsing), the Scholar (researching), and the Task-Oriented Visitor (seeking specific logistics or rights)?  
* **Object detail page hierarchy:** What counts as "Essential" vs. "Premium" data? How is high-level storytelling balanced with granular technical data?  
* **Multi-channel entry points:** Beyond the search bar, how are visitors funneled in via social deep-links, related content, or AI-curated "surprise me" modules?

**Search & Navigation**

* **Search intelligence:** Is the experience evolving from keyword-matching to Semantic/Natural Language Search (e.g., "blue landscapes from the 1920s")?  
* **Faceted filter UX:** What are the current industry standards for filter placement (sidebar vs. top-drawer) and how do they handle complex taxonomies on mobile?  
* **Tagging:** How are non-traditional tags (mood, color, visual similarity) surfaced to facilitate "serendipitous discovery"?

**Data & Interoperability**

* **Provenance \+ ethics:** How is the history of ownership and "Right to Use" communicated to build institutional trust and transparency?  
* **Open Access:** What are the 2026 standards for "Download" workflows (IIIF, high-res) and communicating copyright clearly to users?  
* **Engagement loops:** How are visitors invited to interact with art (Save to Gallery, AI-Discovery, or collaborative annotation)?

---

# **Evaluation Criteria**

Each institution will be audited against four areas:

1. **Object detail page**  
   1. **Core metadata:** Title, Artist, Date, Medium, Dimensions, Credit Line, Accession Number  
   2. **Enhanced context:** Layering of Provenance, exhibition history, and bibliography. Note if there is an "On View" status linked to real-time wayfinding.  
   3. **Technical standards:** IIIF integration \- deep zoom, multi-view, comparison tools.   
   4. **Content layering:** Use of "TL;DR" summaries, AI-generated alt-text, and curator-led long-form narratives, cross-linking to related content

2. **Organization \+ discovery**  
   1. **Entry points:** Beyond the search bar, how are visitors discovering content (ex: curated thematic clusters, "Daily Highlights," or randomizers)  
   2. **Visual \+ emotional discovery:** Options to browse by color, mood, or conceptual similarity (objects linked by theme)  
   3. **The "path forward":** Does the journey end at the object, or are there clear CTAs to "Visit", "See related works", or "Join a tour"?

3. **Search \+ navigation**  
   1. **Search interface:** Evaluation of Basic vs. Advanced (Faceted) presentation. Is there an "AI Assistant" or "Natural Language" input option?  
   2. **Filter UX:** Are filters persistent, mobile-optimized (drawers), and contextual (do filters change based on the search category)?  
   3. **Results handling:** Accuracy of "Did you mean?" prompts and the quality of the "Zero-Results" page (does it suggest alternatives?).

4. **Interaction \+ engagement**  
   1. **Algorithmic recommendations:** "People also viewed" vs. "Visually similar" vs. "Curator's picks."  
   2. **Personalization \+ utility:** Session-based: Quick-save or "Heart" features without an account.  
   3. **Account-based:** Personal galleries, private annotations, and exportable citations.  
   4. **Social \+ educational connectivity:** Ease of sharing to social platforms, links to related education content, or generating Open Access citations.

# FAMSF \- FINAL

**Entry points**  
There are two primary entry points for navigating to FAMSF's collections from the homepage:

* Art navigation header \> Search the Collection  
* "Explore our collections" 'See all collections' button

The “Explore our collections” generic listing options act as secondary entry points since they direct visitors to the department page rather than the collection directly.

Of note, collections content is last to appear on the homepage \- the page prioritizes visit planning and editorial content first. This means visitors who arrive with collection browsing intent have to scroll to find their way in. The collections are also introduced via curatorial areas, which may not be immediately engaging for casual browsers looking for a quick sense of what kind of art the museums hold (a thumbnail gallery may be more compelling as a first impression).

Overall, the limited primary entry points and late-page placement of collection content works against discoverability.

**Collections landing page**  
The landing page does a reasonable job of orienting visitors to the scale and breadth of the collection through introductory text and search bar copy.


The 'Explore by Topic' section is good for cross-linking editorial content with collection artworks, but clicking through takes visitors out of the collection browsing workflow entirely. These topics are also unique to editorial content and can't currently be used to filter artworks within the collection, which is likely to create confusion for visitors who expect them to function as collection filters.

Overall good foundation but lots of opportunities to serve visitors more effectively.

**Search filters \- Basic and advanced**  
The layered approach of basic search on the landing page with a path to advanced filtering helps to serve both casual and research visitors without forcing either into a workflow designed for the other. However, execution falls short for both audiences:

* Basic search has no exposed quick filters or thematic browsing suggestions, leaving casual visitors without a meaningful on-ramp  
* Advanced search lacks introductory text or search guidance  
* Filter options are extremely limited (What, Who, When, Collection) making the experience feel closer to basic search than a true advanced search interface  
* No location/geography filter is a notable absence  
* Only the 'Who' filter dropdown is searchable, meaning visitors must scroll through sometimes very long lists to find what they need

Overall, the current design fails to effectively serve our researcher audience. Filters are too general to support complex search inquiries and the absence of sort and list view options are gaps.

**Collections results**  
The grid layout is easy to skim with a reasonable amount of artwork information surfaced (title, date, artist, and museum location as a proxy for on-view status). Applied filters are clear and easy to remove one at a time.

The general scale of the results page is misaligned with updates made to other parts of the website. Significant white space appears when artwork thumbnails vary in size, which gives the page an unpolished feel at scale.

Functional gaps:

* Application and removal of any single filter requires a full page reload introducing some friction  
* No way to define sort order or criteria  
* No list view, limiting options for researchers who prefer text density and printability  
* No results pages provide no guidance for improving search and no fallback for serendipitous browsing \= a dead-end experience with no suggestions, highlights, or recommendations to keep visitors engaged

**Object detail page \- Image features**  
 No basic image features are present: no deep zoom, no download, no image gallery. A share button allows visitors to post to Facebook or X or copy the page URL.

The absence of image features is a significant failure that undermines the museum's ability to meaningfully share artwork with any audience. For art explorers and researchers alike, being unable to zoom, download, or view multiple images of an object is a fundamental gap.

**Object detail page \- Open Access \+ image rights**  
There are no indicators or text referencing artwork copyright status anywhere on the object detail page for public domain artwork. Artwork in copyright has the copyright near the artwork. As a default, visitors are unable to engage meaningfully with artwork images and without any communication about why, or what their options are, this creates a dead end that feels like an oversight rather than a policy.  
**Object detail page \- Metadata**  
The metadata architecture has a good foundation. Expandable 'Provenance', 'Exhibition History', and 'Bibliography' sections reduce page length on arrival and let visitors choose their own depth of engagement.

Known text formatting issues (paragraph breaks, bold text) currently prevent certain information from being easily parsed, which undermines the value of this content.

Gaps:

* No clickable tags or search terms means object pages function as endpoints rather than nodes \- there is no path forward without returning to search and starting over  
* There's an opportunity to surface more metadata fields that are likely already present in TMS  
* Expandable sections fully expand even when contents is extremely long which can feel like you’re stuff endlessly scrolling to try to collapse the section again  
* No indication of whether an object is part of a parent/child relationship or a series \- a missed opportunity for discovery that also has downstream implications for filtering and search

**Object detail page \- Other content**  
Audio tour content produced via the in-house audio feature is well integrated \- the module clearly shows a thumbnail, audio title, and expandable transcript, and feels native to the page. Audio content produced outside of this workflow (via Bloomberg Connects) doesn't have the same streamlined integration, creating an inconsistent experience depending on which content a visitor encounters.

There is currently no linking to related articles, stories, videos, or educational materials, which means the object detail page is largely self-contained with no editorial context or path to broader programming.  
**Object detail page \- Discovery**  
"Currently on View" and "New Acquisitions" modules appear at the bottom of each object detail page, but these are relatively static and don't provide visitors a meaningful way to continue their current exploration. There are no thematic suggestions such as "By the same artist", no connections by artistic movement or medium, and no logic that responds to what the visitor is actually looking at.

Overall, discovery is underdeveloped and current modules act to surface relatively static institutional artwork rather than helping visitors go deeper into the collection.

**AI \+ Interactive elements**  
No interactive or AI-powered elements are available to support exploration of the digital collection.

# The Met

**Entry points**

Primary entry points include the main website homepage \+ navigation:

* Art \> The Met Collection  
  * Collection artwork highlights module  
  * Art in 3D section

There are several meaningful ways for art explorers and researchers to navigate to the collections area of the website. Art in 3D is a compelling hook for exploratory visitors but could be easily missed at the bottom of the page.

**Collections landing page**

The landing page is a good balance between breadth and direction, allowing visitors get a sense of the collection's scale without being immediately overwhelmed. High-level groupings help orient both art explorers and researchers before diving into individual artworks. There is also a rich “Collection Research and Resources” section at the bottom of the page which links to Open Access.

However:

* The groupings shown are curatorial departments, but this term is never used which is a missed opportunity to build familiarity with how the institution organizes its collection  
* No artwork gallery view means exploratory visitors have no visual way to browse before committing to a search  
* The single introductory sentence is minimal, communicating scale but not character (what makes this collection distinctive and worth searching?)

Overall, the landing page is functional for researchers who arrive with intent but undersells the collection for art explorers who would benefit from more visual and editorial invitation to dive deeper.

**Search filters \- Basic and advanced**

Layered approach where the basic search is on the landing page with a clear path to advanced filtering. This serves both casual and research visitors without forcing either into a workflow designed for the other.

Strengths (Advanced filtering mainly):

* Quick filters (Highlights, On View, Has Image, Open Access) are well-chosen and reduce time-to-result for common visitor tasks  
* The ability to search within in-depth filter lists helps remove friction for navigating larger taxonomy/categories  
* Tooltips on technical filters like "Has Open Access Image" show awareness that not all visitors share the same baseline knowledge \+ helps educate curious explorers  
* No full-page reload required when filters applied allows for visitors to rapidly apply multiple filters without friction of having to wait for the refresh

Gaps:

* Basic search lacks obvious quick filters present on the advanced search page given the layered approach, a text link to direct to a filtered view of artwork (ex: Highlights) is required  
* Narrow search area options (All fields, Artist/Culture, Time, etc.) are useful but lack granularity that specialist researchers might expect  
  * Correction: “All fields” drop down indicates that visitors can search across Artist/culture, description, gallery, accession number, title, and object number). While not including this in the Filter section keeps the interface clean and minimal, it may do a disservice to helping people explore the collection via filters.  
* Search appears to use broad keyword matching across object page text, meaning zero results are more unlikely to occur

Overall well-designed to be a balance between research and art explorers though this naturally under serves researchers who are looking for more nuanced ways to explore art holdings. 

**Collections results**  
Grid layout is clean and easy to skim, consistent text alignment provided visual stability even as thumbnail sizes and orientation varies. Sorting options are robust allowing for sorting by title, date, artist, and accession number in both directions to give visitors meaningful control.

However

* No list view limits how visitors can engage with results \- ex: researchers may prefer density over visual browsing \+ formats which allow for easy printing  
* No on-view indicator in the grid means visitors interested in seeing works in person must use a separate filter rather than being able to scan at a glance introducing some friction to the planning experience  
* No results pages provides minimal guidance for how to improve the search but there’s no fallback to serendipitous browsing (suggestions, highlights gallery module, recommendations based on browsing etc) resulting in a dead-end experience

Overall good for visitors who arrive knowing what they want but could us more support for failed searches and casual browsing to serve art explorers effectively.

**Object detail page \- Image features**  
The image experience is solid- full-screen deep zoom is well-implemented and genuinely useful for close looking with ability to use keyboard and mouse shortcuts creates a very streamlined experience on desktop. The 3D viewing feature, including rotation and AR placement, are meaningful differentiators and serves research, exploratory, and event educational use cases.

The download experience is the main friction point: clicking download opens the image in a new tab and requires a manual save from there. For researchers and open access users who expect a direct download, this extra step is unnecessary.

Overall strong image tooling, with the 3D and AR features standing out as genuinely distinctive. The download flow should be simplified to match the open access intent.

**Object detail page \- Open Access \+ image rights**  
Rights status is clearly surfaced near the image, the tooltip \+ links to the full policy provides accessible context for visitors unfamiliar with open access. The persistent "Research Resources" messaging across all object pages ensures this information isn't buried.

As noted previously, the download flow seems to not fully align with open access push.

Overall, th communication of rights/open access status is clear and supports multiple audiences.

**Object detail page \- Metadata**  
The metadata architecture is thoughtful. Toggle-based sections reduce page length on arrival and let visitors choose their own depth of engagement, a good solution for ensuring art explorers and researchers needs are met. Text formatting (paragraph breaks, bold text) is handled well and makes long-form entries readable

Provenance, exhibition history, signatures/inscriptions/markings, catalogue raisonne and curatorial interpretation are all present making the Met offerings quick robust. Available offerings definitely lend the object detail page as a hub for researchers but the toggle-based sections make it heard to see everything at once or print information efficiently.

Gaps:

* No clickable tags or search terms means object pages function as endpoints rather than nodes \- there is no path forward from the detail page without returning to search and starting over. The exception, Curatorial Department, feels a bit arbitrary and doesn’t serve the most intuitive way for art explorers or researchers to continue to exploration

Overall, metadata depth is strong, especially for researchers. However, the absence of linked tags breaks the chain of discovery for exploratory visitors.

**Object detail page \- Other content**

Related editorial content (Perspectives, Timeline, Publications, Department pieces) is present and organized in columns below the artwork \- for some reason the way it’s structured was not intuitive to me when trying to orient myself to this section of the page. The "free to download" tag on publications is a useful signal.

Notes:

* All editorial content sits below the "More Artwork" block, which means it's easily missed by visitors who don't scroll far enough  
* Audio files (with transcripts) are embedded directly in the page though the module is unnecessarily large and seems to not fully align with branding \- this appears to be limited to audio files associated with audio tours

Overall the content is present but the page architecture can cause some related content to not feel essential and may even be missed entirely by visitors who reach the “More Artwork” block and assume that’s the end of the page. 

**Object detail page \- Discovery**

The "More Artwork" recommendations are rule-based (same gallery, same medium, same time and place, by artist) and clearly labeled making the module transparent and useful. Additional context can help visitors feel more empowered during their explorations.

Limitations:

* Rule-based logic can sometimes make visitors feel like they’re being offered more of the same rather than serendipitous connections  
* While the filmstrip allows visitors to scroll horizontally through “More Artwork” categories, there’s no “Explore all” way for people to see more. Instead they are forced to click on a new artwork to continue to exploration.  
* No clear calls to action toward visiting, programming, or deeper editorial engagement

Overall discovery recommendations are present, intuitive to use, and ensure the object detail page is not a dead end for exploration. 

**AI \+ Interactive elements**  
The most notable interactive feature is the AR "View in Your Space" tool, accessible from within the 3D viewer on select object pages. Visitors can use their phone to place/visualize artwork in their physical environment. Visitors maintain the ability  to zoom and rotate the object within their field of view.

**Design observations**

* Overall clean aesthetic \+ highly skimmable

# The Art Institute of Chicago

**Entry points**

The Art Institute treats its online collection as a primary product with eight distinct entry points from the homepage and navigation. Entry points range from navigation headers, inline text links, a "Plan Your Visit" tool, artwork galleries, and a "New on View" CTA supporting entry points across audiences regardless of whether they’re interested in active or passive browsing.

The "Start Your Discovery" within the sub-navigation is friendly language signaling that the collection is meant to be explored, not just searched.

Overall this is one of the best examples of entry point visibility. The volume and variety of access points reflects institutional commitment to the collection as a digital product, not just an online catalog.

**Collections landing page**

The landing page emphasizes the depth of resources available instead of leading with scale of the collection. Multi-tab structure (Artworks, Publications, Research Center) further signal that this is a research destination.

Exposed filters and artwork gallery alongside simple search mean that visitors don't have to commit to a search before understanding what's available \- especially important since messaging at the top of the page is geared towards researchers and fails to provide context as to the museums artwork offerings or characteristics of the collection.

The featured editorial section at the bottom allows for early cross-linking but its placement at the very bottom of the page limits its impact.

Unique mobile experience: search and quick filters become floating components that preserves functionality on smaller screens without forcing a degraded experience of a long scroll to get to results content. That being said, components are quite small and don’t have a lot of contrast against the artwork thumbnails and could be easily missed.

Overall the multi-tab structure, immediate filter access, and artwork gallery serve visitors well. The featured content section would benefit from moving higher on the page.

**Search filters \- Basic and advanced**

The scrollable suggested themes in the basic search bar are a useful on-ramp for visitors who don't know where to start.

Expansive set of filters \- 13 in-depth filtering categories offer the ability for nuanced searches. Notably, the color wheel filter is unique offering a visually intuitive, mood-based exploration. The "Show Only" filters (multimedia, educational resources, 360 image) allow certain content types to be surfaced earlier and removing the requirement for individual discovery within the object detail page.

Major flaw: Filters are applied one at a time with a full page reload after every selection. This sometimes triggers a human authentication check which is tedious and time consuming. This friction point seems to undercut the strong filter set.

The mobile experience mirrors desktop via a left-hand panel though with the number of filters and the reload after each filter, I’m not sure the quality of the mobile experience.

Overall, the filter depth, unique filters (color wheel and "Show Only" options) are standouts. However, the page-reload-per-filter interaction model is a serious friction point.

**Collections results**  
The masonry layout is visually engaging and helps to avoid empty-space problems that can occur when artwork dimensions vary across the collection. View feels more editorial \+ refined.

Gaps:

* Object information surfaced in the results is minimal \- only title and artist, with no year, medium, or on-view status visible at a glance.  
* No list view limits options for researchers who prefer density and text over visual browsing \+ printability  
* Applied filters are clear and easy to see / remove one at a time  
* Sorting is only accessible via the filters menu, not directly from the results page, which is not the norm and adds an extra step

No results page has a minimal response with not suggestions or alternative paths to continue the exploration. However, reaching a zero-results state is difficult, which suggests strong underlying metadata and search indexing.

The persistent "Featured" section and small-text topic suggestions at the bottom of no-results and results pages offer a path forward, though the text is extremely small and very easily overlooked so effectiveness is undercut.

Overall the masonry layout is a visual strength, but minimal object information, limited sort access, and the ongoing friction of full page reloads undermined the experience.

**Object detail page \- Image features**  
The full-width image centers the artwork as the primary experience before any metadata or context about the artwork appears. Image gallery, full-screen deep zoom with keyboard and mouse shortcuts, and social sharing are all present and functional.

The direct-to-computer download aligns strongly with visitor expectations and ensures a streamlined experience interacting with open access content on the museum website.

360 viewers allows users to use a bar to rotate the object to see different surfaces. Doesn’t allow for zoom or rotating in unique directions \- users are limited to the scrub bard for interacting with the object.

Overall verdict: Solid image experience with a best-in-class download flow. The full-width image treatment effectively prioritizes the artwork. 360 content availability should be verified and surfaced more clearly if present.

**Object detail page \- Open Access \+ image rights**  
The CC0 Public Domain designation is present and clearly placed near the main image. This is easily recognized by researchers but ror visitors who don't already know what CC0 or Public Domain Designation means, the label alone doesn't provide enough context.

The "Show Only: Public Domain" filter is a useful complement, but it serves researchers rather than educating casual visitors about what open access means for them.

Overall the CC0 designation is correct and clearly placed, but the surrounding communication is underdeveloped for non-research audiences.

**Object detail page \- Metadata**  
The metadata hierarchy is solid with core fields (artist, title, date, medium, dimensions, credit line, reference number) exposed by default and deeper research fields (provenance, exhibition history, publication history) collapsed behind an expandable section. This serves both art explorers and researchers without overwhelming either.

IIIF Manifest link it indicates interoperability and positions the AIC as a resource \+ advocate for supporting the exploration of high-resolution artwork images, interactive storytelling, and scholarly collaboration.

Gaps:

* Clickable search terms from metadata are limited meaning object pages aren’t high functioning nodes

Overall, AIC object detail pages offer strong metadata architecture \+ material with good depth for researchers. 

**Object detail page \- Other content**  
The right-hand "Related" column up-levels editorial content to make it visible alongside key artwork metadata, ensuring content isn’t buried at the bottom of the page.

Gaps:

* Videos link out to a separate page, breaking the browsing flow  
* Audio opens in a pop-up with no transcript, creating an accessibility gap

Expandable “Multimedia” and “Educational resources” section allow for all content to be gathered within a single page for easy exploration. “Education resources” section strongly support educators who are looking for a ‘self-service’ experience.

**Object detail page \- Discovery**

The "Explore Further" section offers distinct relationship types which can vary artwork to artwork to provide the most relevant (and I’m assuming popular) exploration paths forward. Examples of these include: Most Similar, Style, Medium, Artist, Date, All Tags. 

Gap:

* Functionality of the “Explore further” section appears to be broken for the artworks I looked at \- clicking through each subheading resulted in the same artwork being displayed.

As previously mentioned, the small-text topic suggestions at the very bottom of the page (artist, time, collection/department) is valuable but too small and visually understated. AIC offers a unique “Recently viewed” module at the bottom of the page which acts to informs the small-text topic suggestions. Visitors are able to “clear your history” to get new suggestions.

# National Gallery of Art (DC)

**Entry points**  
Collection is framed as something to experience and explore. The homepage leans heavily toward art explorers, with interactive hooks like AI-generated mood boards and games sitting alongside more traditional gallery and discovery modules.

Primary entry points from the homepage or main navigation:

* "Artworks" subheading in navigation  
* "Discover our artworks" homepage banner  
* "A few of our favorites" homepage gallery  
* "Download free images" homepage module  
* Games and ArtVibes AI mood board (homepage)

The games and AI mood board as entry points lower the barrier for visitors who wouldn't otherwise engage with a traditional collection interface

Strong entry point visibility with a clear art-explorer orientation \- interactive, ai-powered tools a prominent feature. 

**Collections landing page**  
The landing page boasts eight distinct browse pathways giving visitors choice in how they enter the collection. pathways organized in artwork gallery. Utilized a mix of masonry artwork gallery, scrollable “explore by” sections are geared towards different audiences audience:

* "Explore by Period and Movement" serves art-curious and researcher audiences  
* "Explore by Topic" is pitched at a general audience  
* "Explore by Medium" serves both explorers and researchers  
* The ArtVibes and Artle highlights extend the page's reach to visitors who want something more interactive

Gaps:

* No quick filters alongside the basic search bar means visitors who want to narrow immediately have to commit to a full search first — adding friction for researchers  
* The "Download free images" section doesn't clearly communicate that there are collection artworks available to browse and download — a missed opportunity to convert casually interested visitors into active collection users  
* The "You may also like" module highlights artwork collections that can be explored

The breadth of browse pathways is a strength. 

**Search filters \- Basic and advanced**  
The basic search page provides helpful framing ("Search by title, artist, movement, or accession number") that invites both explorers and researchers, but the only way to access advanced filters is by completing an initial search first. For researchers arriving with a specific intent, this is an unnecessary extra step that treats filtering as a refinement tool rather than a starting point.

Once inside advanced search

* Auto-suggest in both search bars and within individual filter search bars surfaces vocabulary and options that visitors  
* Eight filter categories give researchers meaningful control of how to narrow down their search including “Artist nationality”  
* Breadcrumbing for relevant filters (such as “Place made” is helpful for navigating less familiar location or subject taxonomies. Caveat is that it makes the list of options visually busy

The mobile experience has most significant friction- the top of the page is consumed by text, search bar,  and filters before any artwork is visible when using advanced search. Visitors have to scroll before seeing anything of interest.

Strong filter depth and auto-suggest but lack of exposed filters and poor mobile entry experience, add friction for key audiences.

**Collections results**  
The grid layout aligns artwork at the top of each row but text varies within the bounding box as image dimensions shift. When a tall vertical image appears alongside smaller horizontal works, the white space becomes visually significant and alignment breaks down noticeably.

The list view serves researchers who want to skim or print results. The amount of metadata surfaced in the grid (title, artist, year, medium, accession number) is also above average.

The on-view status and location are present but rendered in the smallest text on the page — even with bolding, this is likely missed by visitors scanning quickly.

The download icon for open access artworks in the results grid surfaces a key capability at the point of discovery rather than requiring visitors to navigate to an object page to find it.

The no-results page is a dead end with no suggestions, alternative search prompts, or fallback browse options.

**Object detail page \- Image features**  
Layout prioritizes information alongside the artwork which may serve researchers well but may feel less immersive for art explorers who want to spend time with the image before engaging with metadata.

The robust visual description is an important accessibility feature and extends the searchability of the collection beyond standard metadata fields.

The pop-out zoom tool, filmstrip image gallery, and direct-to-computer download for open access works. The download experience is especially streamlined.

**Object detail page \- Open Access \+ image rights**  
Rights communication is clear, contextual, and actionable. The open access symbol is accompanied by plain language, a link to the full policy, and a contact option for image usage questions which covering the needs of both casual visitors and professional researchers. Information is displayed below the artwork gallery instead of immediately along the main artwork image.

**Object detail page \- Metadata**  
The clickable credit line and Wikidata ID signal attention to a technically sophisticated audience.

The page hierarchy creates a discoverability problem for the "Artwork History and Notes" section which sits below both the "More About This Artwork" section and expandable introductory text. This means that visitors must scroll the majority of the page before encountering research-critical information (provenance, exhibition history, and bibliography).

Both major metadata sections are collapsed by default, which keeps the page manageable on arrival but means key information requires active participation to surface. The formatting when expanded is good — scannable and well-structured.

**Object detail page \- Other content**  
The audio player integrated immediately after the artwork description ensuring that it’s content that is quickly surfaced to visitors. The transcript panel pops out making transcript easily read but is more disruptive than an inline toggle/expansion.

The "View Tour Stop" link directs visitors away from the object detail page breaks the browsing flow.

The "Look Closer" module takes up a large amount of real-estate and initially feels to be immersive but fails when only text is available without detailed shows of the artwork .

The "More About This Artwork" video/article section placement after the audio player and before the core metadata creates a slightly uneven content hierarchy which can negatively impact overall visitor experience.

**Object detail page \- Discovery**  
The "You May Be Interested In" module with three toggle sections (By the Same Artist, In the Same Style, In the Same Gallery) provides an instance of rule-based discovery. The "In the Same Style" and "In the Same Gallery" options in particular enable broader exploration beyond the current artist.

The toggle structure gives visitors agency in how they navigate onward, which is preferable to a single undifferentiated recommendation grid. However, this can limit the potential for truly unexpected connections.

**AI \+ Interactive elements**  
The NGA has two prominent interactive features via ArtLens:

* ArtVibes is a very visitor-friendly word picker (adjective, noun, verb) removes the blank-page problem by giving visitors a low-stakes, playful starting point. The gallery of past generated boards means visitors who don't want to create their own content still get value from the tool. Save and share without login removes a common barrier to engagement. The constraint of no free text input keeps the experience focused, though it may frustrate visitors who have something specific in mind.  
* Artle is pitched at a more specialist audience — identifying an artist from four artworks requires art historical knowledge that general visitors may lack, which limits its reach. The end result, however, does good discovery work: surfacing written context about the artist and a path to their full collection is a meaningful payoff that extends engagement beyond the game itself.

# Cleveland Museum of Art

**Entry points**  
The primary access point is via the navigation \- "Search the Collection" subheading under “Art”. Several entry points are either secondary or inconsistently positioned:

* "About the Collection" in navigation is a secondary entry point requiring an additional click  
* "Search the Collection" appearing as the last quick link option within the main site search buries as a primary destination  
* The AI tool set, thematic gallery, and Open Access module all appear in the last third of the homepage, requiring significant scroll commitment before a visitor encounters them

The AI collection tool set offer distinctive entry points and signals institutional commitment to AI-powered engagement. However, grouping Open Access within the AI tool set feels like a miscategorization since its not an interactive tool and visitors looking for it may not think to look there.  
**Collections landing page**  
The landing page has no introductory text, no framing of what the collection contains or what makes it distinctive, and no heading (the search bar copy effectively is the header). For researchers arriving with intent this is functional, but for general audiences or visitors who are unsure what they're looking for, it's can be an abrupt and sterile entry.

Exposed basic filters alongside the search bar give researchers immediate control, but lacks orientation text or browse pathways for non-researcher audiences. The masonry gallery below provides some visual browsing capability but still lacks curation to help guide exploration in a meaningful way.

**Search filters \- Basic and advanced**  
Strengths:

* Consistent implementation of auto-suggest within the search bar and individual filter fields  
* The "Filtered By" dropdown scopes search terms to specific fields (artist name, title, medium, description) giving researchers precise control  
* The date slider with text input fallback serves both casual and precise date-based searches  
* The "History" section of advanced search (covering credit line, exhibition history, provenance, and rights) is robust, offering research audiences opportunities to search more industry fields

Gaps:

* Dropdown filters only allow one selection at a time \- no multi-select for departments, for example . This is a meaningful limitation for researchers building complex queries  
* The advanced search panel appearing within the webpage and pushing content down creates an unwieldy scroll on both desktop and especially mobile

**Collections results**  
The masonry layout with minimal white space creates a dense, visually busy results page. The combination of masonry layout and lots of exposed metadata (title, location, date, accession number, open access status) veers toward information overload.

That said, the list view with its density of information (thumbnail, title, artist, accession number, medium, dimensions, credit line, rights status) is great and provides a print ready page without a separate export function.

The open access status icon is surfaced on the results grid though it relies on visitors recognizing the symbol. The sorting options are robust, though "Close" appearing alongside sort choices rather than functioning clearly as a UI dismiss icon is a confusing but otherwise a small issue.

**Object detail page \- Image features**  
The visual text description is a great feature but can be hard to locate as it overlays the main artwork images and disappears with inactivity.

Standard deep zoom and keyboard shortcuts are present and functional. The "Alternative Views"  button is not an obvious way for people to access the artwork gallery. It adds unnecessary steps and can easily be overlooked due to lack of visual hook.

**Object detail page \- Open Access \+ image rights**  
The CMA's rights communication is very effective and explains exactly what visitors can do with a public domain image to remove ambiguity ("You can copy, modify, and distribute this work, all without asking permission").

When an object is under copyright, the download button remains visible but a pop-up explains the limitation and offers three clear alternatives (metadata download, image order, open access FAQs), avoiding a dead end while keeping visitors informed.

**Object detail page \- Metadata**  
The bilingual title display (English \+ language of origin) is a thoughtful inclusion and sign of cultural respect.

The persistent thumbnail module that follows visitors as they scroll is useful and helps maintains artwork context without having to scroll to the very top of the page.

The "Did You Know?" education module is a fun way to provide additional context related to an artwork but wonder if there’s a better place to include the module. The inclusion of "Cite This Page" as a collapsed field great for researchers/academics.

**Object detail page \- Other content**  
The "Did You Know?" module surfaces accessible, education-focused content in a way most peer institutions don't attempt at the object page level. The embedded video in a collapsed field is functional but can hurt discoverability, especially for art explorer audiences who may be less likely to explore the researcher focused collapsed section. .

**Object detail page \- Discovery**  
The "See Also" module with examples of artwork related by medium, collection, and department allows for continued discovery of related artwork. The "Visually Similar by AI" feature shares a list of artwork generated using Ai that can allow for more unique explorations.

While not strictly collections, the Shop the CMA Store module helps surface related merchandise at a moment of high engagement . It appears only when relevant merchandise items exist.

"Cover Record" is a useful signal for researchers but the language feels internal. More visitor-friendly alternatives could be used to help better communicate with general audiences. 

**AI \+ Interactive elements**  
Most substantial institutional investment in AI tools as a way to bridge the gap between a visitor’s personal ife and the online collection:

* Extend the Art invites visitors to generate new artwork from their own ideas, creating an interactive collection experience. The gallery of visitor-generated content means the tool has value even for visitors who don't want to create their own.  
* Share Your View allows visitors to match their own photograph to collection artworks to create a more personal connection between a visitor and the museum's collection.  
* ArtLens for Slack targets workplace audiences and allows for a fun / low effort way to explore the online collections passively.

# FAMSF \- FINAL

**Entry points**  
There are two primary entry points for navigating to FAMSF's collections from the homepage:

* Art navigation header \> Search the Collection  
* "Explore our collections" 'See all collections' button

The “Explore our collections” generic listing options act as secondary entry points since they direct visitors to the department page rather than the collection directly.

Of note, collections content is last to appear on the homepage \- the page prioritizes visit planning and editorial content first. This means visitors who arrive with collection browsing intent have to scroll to find their way in. The collections are also introduced via curatorial areas, which may not be immediately engaging for casual browsers looking for a quick sense of what kind of art the museums hold (a thumbnail gallery may be more compelling as a first impression).

Overall, the limited primary entry points and late-page placement of collection content works against discoverability.

**Collections landing page**  
The landing page does a reasonable job of orienting visitors to the scale and breadth of the collection through introductory text and search bar copy.


The 'Explore by Topic' section is good for cross-linking editorial content with collection artworks, but clicking through takes visitors out of the collection browsing workflow entirely. These topics are also unique to editorial content and can't currently be used to filter artworks within the collection, which is likely to create confusion for visitors who expect them to function as collection filters.

Overall good foundation but lots of opportunities to serve visitors more effectively.

**Search filters \- Basic and advanced**  
The layered approach of basic search on the landing page with a path to advanced filtering helps to serve both casual and research visitors without forcing either into a workflow designed for the other. However, execution falls short for both audiences:

* Basic search has no exposed quick filters or thematic browsing suggestions, leaving casual visitors without a meaningful on-ramp  
* Advanced search lacks introductory text or search guidance  
* Filter options are extremely limited (What, Who, When, Collection) making the experience feel closer to basic search than a true advanced search interface  
* No location/geography filter is a notable absence  
* Only the 'Who' filter dropdown is searchable, meaning visitors must scroll through sometimes very long lists to find what they need

Overall, the current design fails to effectively serve our researcher audience. Filters are too general to support complex search inquiries and the absence of sort and list view options are gaps.

**Collections results**  
The grid layout is easy to skim with a reasonable amount of artwork information surfaced (title, date, artist, and museum location as a proxy for on-view status). Applied filters are clear and easy to remove one at a time.

The general scale of the results page is misaligned with updates made to other parts of the website. Significant white space appears when artwork thumbnails vary in size, which gives the page an unpolished feel at scale.

Functional gaps:

* Application and removal of any single filter requires a full page reload introducing some friction  
* No way to define sort order or criteria  
* No list view, limiting options for researchers who prefer text density and printability  
* No results pages provide no guidance for improving search and no fallback for serendipitous browsing \= a dead-end experience with no suggestions, highlights, or recommendations to keep visitors engaged

**Object detail page \- Image features**  
 No basic image features are present: no deep zoom, no download, no image gallery. A share button allows visitors to post to Facebook or X or copy the page URL.

The absence of image features is a significant failure that undermines the museum's ability to meaningfully share artwork with any audience. For art explorers and researchers alike, being unable to zoom, download, or view multiple images of an object is a fundamental gap.

**Object detail page \- Open Access \+ image rights**  
There are no indicators or text referencing artwork copyright status anywhere on the object detail page for public domain artwork. Artwork in copyright has the copyright near the artwork. As a default, visitors are unable to engage meaningfully with artwork images and without any communication about why, or what their options are, this creates a dead end that feels like an oversight rather than a policy.  
**Object detail page \- Metadata**  
The metadata architecture has a good foundation. Expandable 'Provenance', 'Exhibition History', and 'Bibliography' sections reduce page length on arrival and let visitors choose their own depth of engagement.

Known text formatting issues (paragraph breaks, bold text) currently prevent certain information from being easily parsed, which undermines the value of this content.

Gaps:

* No clickable tags or search terms means object pages function as endpoints rather than nodes \- there is no path forward without returning to search and starting over  
* There's an opportunity to surface more metadata fields that are likely already present in TMS  
* Expandable sections fully expand even when contents is extremely long which can feel like you’re stuff endlessly scrolling to try to collapse the section again  
* No indication of whether an object is part of a parent/child relationship or a series \- a missed opportunity for discovery that also has downstream implications for filtering and search

**Object detail page \- Other content**  
Audio tour content produced via the in-house audio feature is well integrated \- the module clearly shows a thumbnail, audio title, and expandable transcript, and feels native to the page. Audio content produced outside of this workflow (via Bloomberg Connects) doesn't have the same streamlined integration, creating an inconsistent experience depending on which content a visitor encounters.

There is currently no linking to related articles, stories, videos, or educational materials, which means the object detail page is largely self-contained with no editorial context or path to broader programming.  
**Object detail page \- Discovery**  
"Currently on View" and "New Acquisitions" modules appear at the bottom of each object detail page, but these are relatively static and don't provide visitors a meaningful way to continue their current exploration. There are no thematic suggestions such as "By the same artist", no connections by artistic movement or medium, and no logic that responds to what the visitor is actually looking at.

Overall, discovery is underdeveloped and current modules act to surface relatively static institutional artwork rather than helping visitors go deeper into the collection.

**AI \+ Interactive elements**  
No interactive or AI-powered elements are available to support exploration of the digital collection.
