# **Project Context** {#project-context}

This document synthesizes findings from stakeholder discovery interviews conducted as part of FAMSF's 2026 Collections Project — a strategic initiative to modernize how the museum's digital collection is managed, maintained, and experienced by the public.

The interviews were designed to surface pain points, priorities, and aspirations from the curatorial staff who rely on the collections website (and related products) daily, and whose audiences — researchers, educators, and the general public — depend on it for access to the collection.  
---

# **Methodology** {#methodology}

**Interview format:** Small group sessions, 45–60 minutes per group   
**Interview dates:** March 23 – April 8, 2026 

**Departments interviewed:** Contemporary Art; American Art; Achenbach Foundation for Graphic Arts; Arts of Africa, Oceania \+ the Americas; European Paintings \+ Decorative Arts; Costume \+ Textile Arts; Collections Information \+ Digital Assets (CIDA); Education; Curatorial Fellows; Program \+ Strategy / Curatorial Liaison (written survey)

**Key learning goals:**

* What problems do curators, researchers, or educators have with the current collections website?  
* What functionality or features matter most to you and the people you serve?  
* What problems or frustrations exist today that we have an opportunity to solve?  
* What functionality must never break?  
* Which peer institutions do you admire as a digital north star?  
* What frustrates users most often?  
* What would success look like for this project?  
* What operational or engineering pain points slow the team down?  
* What opportunities are we currently missing?

**Output:** Synthesis report, a compiled list of aspirational institutional examples (see Supplemental A), and an initial feature wishlist (see Feature Candidates and Supplemental B).

# **Executive Summary** {#executive-summary}

## Themes {#themes}

Eight themes emerged consistently across all five interview groups:

1. Search is broken and must be fixed  
2. Images are the single most universally requested improvement  
3. The object detail page is a critical entry and discovery point  
4. Departmental organization is "insider baseball" to the public  
5. eMuseum can't be decommissioned without a functional replacement  
6. AI is more welcome on the backend, cautious on the front-end  
7. Content connectivity and circular navigation are underbuilt  
8. The current digital collection experience undersells the institution

## Features {#features}

Potential feature candidates emerging from the interviews have been tentatively broken into “High priority” and “Lower priority”. See [Feature Candidates](#feature-candidates) for the full list.

* General clarity on primary audiences for our collections site.

# **Emerging Themes** {#emerging-themes}

The following themes emerged consistently across the interview groups.

1. **Search is broken and must be fixed first:** Search was flagged as a significant pain point. Issues span unreliable results, missing works, no intuitive date-range filtering, confusing advanced search UI, and attribution information not surfacing properly in result thumbnails. The Achenbach group specifically noted that current display issues make it impossible to get an overview of the strength and expanse of the collection. The Costume \+ Textiles group added that the absence of geography and material/technique filters is particularly troublesome for their collection, as a good portion of their objects are by anonymous makers. Isabella further advocated for geography and culture as two distinct filters, noting that how the museum handles geography internally requires cross-departmental alignment before it can be meaningfully surfaced to the public. The Curatorial Fellows provided a striking illustration of the current search gap: searching "English" on the public site returns 8 results, while the same search in eMuseum returns 206\. CIDA surfaced that the website currently defaults to Getty TGN local-language place names rather than the museum's preferred internal terms, creating inconsistencies between what curators see in TMS and what the public sees.  
2. **Images are the single most universally requested improvement:** Across all curatorial groups, high-resolution, zoomable, downloadable, multi-view images were the top feature request. Staff are currently resorting to Wikipedia, screenshots, TMS, eMuseum, and other workarounds to get access to and download images. The Education team noted that strong zoom functionality was a capability the FAMSF website had in previous iterations that has since been lost. This is particularly meaningful for engaging educators and students who cannot be present in the gallery. The CIDA team provided important context that approximately 70% of the collection already has photography at sufficient resolution for deep zoom, and multi-angle photography is already being captured in current workflows. However, tests need to be done to confirm whether the resolution truly supports IIF investment at this time.   
3. **The object detail page is a critical entry and discovery point:** Object detail pages should function as a one-stop shop and a way to encourage deeper engagement with the institution. Examples of robust object detail pages included features such as an image gallery, exhibition history, related works (by artist, medium, subject matter, etc.), and linked content (audio tours, articles, essays, press releases). The Education team specifically asked for clear labeling of content sources \- whether the current gallery label or a web essay \- so that teachers can trust what they are using for lesson planning. CIDA highlighted that TMS holds significantly richer data than the public website currently reflects. Multiple fields such as multiple titles, subclassifications, object names, GeoXrefs with hierarchy, constituent nationality/origin, uncertainty qualifiers, style and period attributes, exist in the database but are not pushed to the public record. Closing this data-to-web gap is a prerequisite for many of the improvements stakeholders are requesting.  
4. **Departmental organization is insider baseball:** It was generally acknowledged that organizing the public-facing collection by internal administrative departments can be confusing. Art-historical periods, movements, medium, and geography could be more intuitive to general audiences. Changes to organization will require significant alignment across curatorial areas. Additionally, the current single-department structure doesn’t allow for the nuanced understanding that an object can belong to multiple departments (e.g., African textiles stewarded by C+T, textile works stewarded by African Art).   
5. **eMuseum cannot be decommissioned without a functional replacement:** While the majority of teams utilize eMuseum opportunistically, the Achenbach team is dependent on it within their current workflows. eMuseum provides: thematic browsing, image downloads, filtered searches, credit line queries, personal research packages. Fellows also rely on eMuseum as an informal undo tool, copying pre-edit data when TMS changes cannot be reversed.  
6. **AI is more welcome on the backend, cautious on the front-end:** The clearest AI consensus is that curators are more open to backend applications (ex: auto-tagging) while front-facing applications are met with more skepticism/concern. The AOA team specifically raised critical concerns about Eurocentric bias and the absence of indigenous knowledge in current AI systems. Costume and Textiles raised additional concerns around “ai hallucination”, citation integrity, and the accountability gap such features would introduce. The Education team was among the more enthusiastic voices, but with a specific and nuanced application in mind: an AI tool that scales verified, high-quality content to age-appropriate reading levels for student audiences — dramatically reducing the labor educators currently spend adapting material. Emily also raised an important design consideration: AI features should be opt-in or opt-out, particularly for student users, to give educators control over how AI mediates their students' learning.  
7. **Content connectivity and circular navigation are underbuilt:** Stakeholders across groups want the site to feel interconnected \- click an artist name, get all their works; click a medium, get a filtered result; follow an object into its exhibition history, into related press releases, into an essay, into an audio tour. The current site is largely siloed and does not support this kind of exploration. The Curatorial Fellows reinforced this with a concrete example: FAMSF articles and editorial content exist that reference specific objects, but there is no link from the object page to that content. Fellows also raised the possibility of dedicated collector and dealer pages — navigable entities with biographical context and associated object listings, similar to what the Getty offers.  
8. **The collection undersells the institution:** Multiple stakeholders noted that the true depth and breadth of the collection is invisible to the public. The website as a vehicle for communicating institutional stature and scholarly standing is a major unrealized opportunity, especially for the Achenbach department who boasts one of the largest works-on-paper collections in the US. The Curatorial Liaison survey added two important dimensions: first, that donors are a specifically underserved audience (ex: the Anderson family has noted that they cannot find the contents of their gift on the website) and second, that the museum should more actively leverage its distinct collection strengths and institutional identity online. Finally, they noted that a provenance statement on the collections pages is industry standard. The Curatorial Fellows echoed this theme. Felix noted that one of the first things outside visitors mention is their interest in Alma Spreckels' collection, and that surfacing founder and major collector stories — tied to San Francisco's own civic history — is an unrealized opportunity to connect the institution to the city it inhabits.

# **Feature Candidates** {#feature-candidates}

## High Priority / Potential “Must Haves” {#high-priority-/-potential-“must-haves”}

* High-res images  
* Downloadable images  
* Interactive (deep zoom) images  
* Multiple images per object record (details, verso, scale, installation views)  
* More intuitive / visible advance search interface  
* Advanced search expanded: donor/collector, credit line, accession number, acquisition date, identity filters, geography (nesting hierarchy), culture (distinct from geography), materials, and technique  
* Dynamic filtering (only display options with matching results)  
* Attribution qualifiers visible in search result thumbnails  
* Paragraph breaks from TMS reflected accurately on the public website  
* Expanded collection area filters beyond 8 curatorial departments (e.g., photography, drawings, prints as distinct browse options)  
* Parent-child record structure for portfolios, books, and multi-part works  
* AI-powered discovery / search suggestions drawing from verified FAMSF content (publications, labels, essays)  
* More robust Tombstone information pushed from TMS (multiple titles, object name, subclassifications, alt text)  
* Alternate accession numbers searchable on the public website  
* Filter reset / unstick bug fix (applied filters should reliably clear when deselected)  
* Rights statement icons on object records (clickable through to rightstatements.org)  
* Image request link on object detail page (pre-filled with accession number)  
* Open access image download button (for qualifying objects)  
* Content source label on object detail page (e.g., "current gallery label," "web essay")

## Lower Priority / Potential “Should Haves” and “Could Haves” {#lower-priority-/-potential-“should-haves”-and-“could-haves”}

* Downloadable / shareable search results  
* Date-range filter (user-entered year span)  
* Data accuracy disclaimer on object records with a contact email for error reporting (modeled on Art Institute of Chicago and Nelson-Atkins Museum)  
* Gallery-location filter (filter by which specific gallery an object is currently on view in)  
* Increased results per page  
* Object label text displayed by default (not collapsed)  
* Hyperlinked metadata fields on object pages triggering filtered searches  
* Provenance statement on the collections pages (industry standard)  
* Gallery/banner images on collection landing pages (updated with current installations)  
* Reorganized collection landing page: chronological and movement-based navigation in addition to departmental (example, final content still to be decided)  
* Citation copy-paste generator / tooltip  
* Related/similar works module on object detail pages (thematic, AI-assisted, or metadata-driven)  
* Exhibition history as a searchable field  
* Installation views linked to object records (historic and recent)  
* Artist pages with dedicated essays and full work listings  
* Heilbrunn-style scholarly essay series linked to object records and collection areas  
* Historic press releases and marketing materials on object/artist pages  
* Personal gallery / favorites / account with research album  
* Video/trailer integration on object detail pages  
* 3D scanned objects  
* Object scale representation relative to human figure  
* "What to see in an hour" / curated visit path feature on collection landing pages  
* Visit planning / concierge experience (AI-assisted, visitor specifies who they are and what they want to see)  
* Educational resources tag in search results and on object detail pages (linking to curriculum and lesson plans)  
* AI reading-level adaptation of verified content for student audiences (opt-in/opt-out for educators)  
* Upcoming programs dynamically populating on collection/department landing pages  
* Indigenous tagging framework (community-based, for relevant AOA records)  
* AI-assisted backend tagging, constituent linking, and bibliographic deduplication  
* AI-assisted image QA batch identification (e.g., flagging uncropped color cards)  
* GeoXref hierarchy display (nested geography paths: country → region → city)  
* Style / period / subject attributes as searchable and displayable fields (requires data entry program)  
* Constituent GeoXrefs (artist nationality, place of birth) as searchable filters  
* Uncertainty qualifiers ("possibly by X") surfaced on object detail pages  
* Jump-to navigation bars on collection and sub-collection pages  
* Scholarly long-form entries on select highlights  
* Print-friendly object records  
* Editorial review workflow / data vetting process before records go live  
* Audit of existing content resources (publications, labels, essays) for web reuse

* "Most viewed" or recently added module on collection landing page  
* Playful discovery tools (random trait shuffle, visual prompt search)  
* Two-column layout for provenance, exhibition history, and bibliography on object detail pages (reduces scrolling)

# **Individual Interview Summaries** {#individual-interview-summaries}

## Contemporary Art {#contemporary-art}

**Participants:** Claudia Schmuckli (Curator-in-Charge, Contemporary Art and Programming), Micaela Duran (Curatorial Cataloging Fellow)

**Summary**   
The conversation covered search failures, the mismatch between internal departmental categories and how the public thinks about art, and the need for a more dynamic and visually engaging entry point to our collection. Micaela highlighted broken HTML formatting, missing date-range filters, and the misalignment between TMS records and what surfaces on the website. Both stakeholders talk through the object detail page at length, advocating for image galleries with multiple views, embedded video/trailers for time-based media, links to exhibition history, and related works. Neither relies on eMuseum; their work centers on TMS. In follow-up, Micaela also recommended adding a data accuracy disclaimer to object records, noting that the Art Institute of Chicago and Nelson-Atkins Museum both include notices alerting researchers that information may not be current, with a contact email for reporting errors. Given that fellows are actively updating Tier 3 information over the next two years, she flagged this as a practical safeguard. She also shared an example of a Collections Search Guide (a dedicated help page explaining how to effectively use the database) and noted that some peer institutions allow users to download and share search results directly, which is a valuable tool for researchers.

**Key Themes**

* Search results are unreliable and not oriented around user intent  
* Departmental taxonomy (by medium/geography) is invisible and confusing to the public — art-historical periods and movements would serve visitors better  
* Object detail page needs richer image galleries, video integration, and links to exhibition history  
* The highlights section lacks consistency, strategic curation, and clarity of purpose  
* The breadth and depth of the collection is dramatically undersold online  
* Object records should carry a data accuracy disclaimer with a contact for error reporting, given ongoing Tier 3 updates  
* A Collections Search Guide and downloadable/shareable search results would support researcher workflows

**Key Quotes**  
"Frankly speaking, a lot of our visitors and a lot of researchers don't care which department a work lives in. They will have very different search criteria and they will very likely not find a work of art because they do not know how to find it." — Claudia

"I still feel it's completely undersold. We have amazing collections and it's just not clear to people that we do to the extent that we do… across all departments, I don't think people have a true grasp of the importance of those collections because we don't communicate it." — Claudia

"Research showed that the majority of the times when people are working with museum collections, their first interaction is with a specific object record that they found through Google — not even necessarily going through a museum collections database." — Micaela

## American Art {#american-art}

**Participants:** Ana Rodrigues (Curatorial Fellow, American Art), Timothy Burgard (Distinguished Senior Curator Curator-in-Charge of American Art), Emma Acker (Curator, American Art)

**Summary**   
The American Art team brought a strong institutional critique alongside detailed feature requests. Tim noted a "widespread perception" that FAMSF lags behind peer art museums digitally, and that he sometimes Googles collection objects rather than using the site directly. Emma stressed lack of accuracy in the current collection experience; incomplete holdings, missing images, and search results that don't surface all works by a given artist. Ana emphasized how confusing the departmental structure is to outside users. All three aligned on the object detail page needs major updates including feature improvements such as print-friendly records, copy-paste citations, the label text un-collapsed by default, related/similar works, and a collection search bar embedded on the object page itself. This department advocated for rethinking collection organization using chronology and art movements rather than administrative departments.

**Key Themes**

* Search accuracy and completeness is the baseline — results must reflect the full collection  
* Internal departmental taxonomy is confusing and should be replaced or supplemented with public-friendly categories (chronology, movement, medium)  
* The object detail page is poorly organized, and lacks a print-friendly format  
* The highlights section is inconsistently curated, often features works not on view, and lacks strategic rationale  
* User research and usability testing should inform decisions — staff shortcuts are not a proxy for public experience

**Key Quotes**  
"There's a widespread perception that we're not up to the level of our other nine major American art museums. That is a perennial question — the feeling that we're not even matching our peers, let alone surpassing them." — Timothy

"It feels like you're opening a drawer in the deepest dungeons of the British Museum and there are all these bugs in amber stuck on needles. 'This is the way we've always done it.' And it's like, 'Well, why? And who cares?'" — Timothy

## Achenbach Foundation for Graphic Arts {#achenbach-foundation-for-graphic-arts}

**Participants:** Furio Rinaldi (Curator in Charge, Achenbach), Natalia Lauricella (Curator of Prints & Drawings), Sally Martin Katz (Associate Curator of Photography), Alice Timmins (Curatorial Projects Coordinator)

**Summary**   
The Achenbach team was the strongest case for maintaining eMuseum functionality as it’s such a critical part of their current workflows – unlike other departments which tend to be more opportunistic with its use. Because works on paper cannot be on permanent display, the online collection is their primary public-facing interface, making it uniquely high-stakes. eMuseum's value to them is specific: thematic/visual browsing across a massive collection, image downloads at presentation quality, filtering by credit line and accession number, and the ability to save personal research packages. Walk-throughs of other collections highlighted advanced search, parent-child portfolio display, and integration of installation shots and historic press releases. Furio closed noting that his vision for our digital collections experience to be a clean, professional, reassuring site where you immediately feel confident you'll find what you need.

**Key Themes**

* eMuseum cannot be decommissioned until the public website fully replicates its search, browse, and image download functionality  
* The Achenbach's large and largely un-displayed collection makes online discoverability existentially important  
* Parent-child record structure is critical for portfolios, sketchbooks, and illustrated books  
* Exhibition history data in TMS is valuable but not searchable — this is a missed opportunity  
* AI most welcome for backend cataloging, tagging, and constituent enrichment — not necessarily as a chatbot  
* Credit line / donor searchability is important for collection research and legacy gift documentation

**Key Quotes**  
"I actually don't know if we could do our jobs without eMuseum, to be perfectly honest." — Natalia

"The history of exhibitions — we are investing so much in populating tier three with all the exhibition history. What is the point if this information cannot be searched?" — Furio

"I would love for our website to be recognized for being effective, professional, clean, simple — a reassuring point of arrival for everyone. You open the website and the first reaction should be, 'Oh, thank God it's FAMSF. I know I'm going to find what I need and it's easy.'" — Furio

## Arts of Africa, Oceania \+ the Americas (AOA) {#arts-of-africa,-oceania-+-the-americas-(aoa)}

**Participants:** Christina Hellmich (Curator in Charge, AOA), Hillary Olcott (Curator of Arts of the Americas), Natasha Becker (Curator of African Art)

**Summary**   
One of the strongest points made during this conversation was that while the three sub-collections share an administrative umbrella, it is essential that each (Arts of Oceania, Arts of Africa, Arts of the Americas) have its own distinct identity and presentation on the website. When asked about how they interact with collections related systems (website, eMuseum, Media Studio), Hillary mentioned that she still utilizes a “retired” tool called 4D as it has valuable legacy data, particularly for NAGPRA-related research, that is not accessible anywhere else. She emphasized that data continuity across historical database migrations matters deeply to their work. Neither Hillary nor Christina uses eMuseum; TMS is their primary tool. The group noted that their collection pages used to be more information-rich but have been stripped back. All three raised fundamental questions around what values and audiences we are designing the collections experience for with Hillary advocated firmly for getting the basics right before pursuing advanced interactivity. 

**Key Themes**

* Sub-collection identities (Africa, Oceania, Americas) must be distinct and clearly navigable — not just administrative units  
* Institutional narrative, collection history, and robust about-pages have been eroded and need restoring  
* The collection pages currently feel static — they should convey dynamism and change (rotating gallery images, updated content)  
* A fundamental values question must be answered: are we serving casual visitors, researchers, or communities without in-person access?  
* AI raises significant concerns around Eurocentric bias and the absence of indigenous knowledge frameworks  
* Community-informed tagging (indigenous cataloging systems) should be part of the redesign conversation

**Key Quotes**  
"We are really intent on their individual identities — that they are presented as distinct collection areas and have their own identities for Oceanic art, Arts of Africa, and Arts of the Americas." — Christina

"Do we think of our collections as research collections? Do we think of our collections as serving communities that might not have access to our collections in person? If somebody lives in Madagascar and they want to look at Madagascan objects in our collection, what kind of experience are they having?" — Natasha

"I would advocate for putting basic useful information in the foreground again first." — Hillary

## Costume \+ Textile Arts {#costume-+-textile-arts}

**Participants:** Laura Camerlengo (Curator in Charge, Costume \+ Textile Arts), Talia Spielholz (Assistant Curator), Sofia Kane (Cataloging Curatorial Fellow)

**Summary**   
The Costume \+ Textiles team brought a unique perspective as their collection spans approximately 20,000 objects across 120+ countries and cultures, the vast majority made by anonymous makers. For them, this makes the "who" filter largely irrelevant and elevates geography and material/technique as the most critical search parameters (neither of which is currently surfaced in the advanced search today). Laura and Talia both raised the cross-departmental nature of their holdings as a structural problem: textiles live in multiple curatorial areas, and the current single-department tagging means objects are routinely invisible to researchers who don't know to look in the right silo. The team noted that TMS Media Studio has largely replaced their need for eMuseum. On the object detail page, the team generally liked the existing format but identified paragraph breaks/formatting issues as the most immediate and foundational fix needed. Laura raised the need for a more formal editorial review process before records go live, resulting in low-quality photography, formatting errors, and outdated content being publicly visible. This was generally acknowledged to be beyond the scope of the current project. The team was the most skeptical of AI among all groups, with Sofia articulating concerns about hallucination, academic citation integrity, and the accountability burden on curatorial staff for errors generated by AI they did not create.

**Key Themes**

* Geography and material/technique are the most critical missing search filters for this collection  
* Cross-departmental object tagging is needed — many objects legitimately belong to multiple collection areas  
* The "what" filter is inconsistent with TMS classifications and not useful in its current form  
* Paragraph breaks from TMS must be reflected on the public website — this is the most immediately impactful fix  
* An editorial review/vetting process for web records is absent and needed  
* The highlights section is outdated and difficult to keep current given staffing constraints  
* AI raises significant concerns: “hallucination”, false information, citation integrity, and accountability  
* A robust, self-serve collections page would reduce staff time spent fielding inquiries

**Key Quotes**  
"If \[the collections page\] is built up and has a lot of good information, it ultimately ends up helping staff because people are not emailing the teams for questions because they can find it themselves. So it's ultimately a time save." — Laura

"The number one most important thing is to have the paragraph breaks that are reflected in TMS also be reflected on the website… it's a pretty foundational thing for the information we're presenting." — Sofia

"As soon as AI is in there, that really undermines the public trust — we're no longer trusted to give them accurate information." — Sofia

## Collections Information and Digital Assets (CIDA) {#collections-information-and-digital-assets-(cida)}

**Participants:** Kathleen Forrest (Collections Information Manager), Britta Traub (Rights Manager), Dahlia Terrio (Collections Information Specialist)

**Summary**  
This interview included a systematic review of TMS fields that exist in the database but are not currently surfaced on the public website. Discussions of photography inventory, open access planning, and rights display were also included. Kathleen walked through a prioritized list of unpublished fields: multiple titles, subclassifications, object names, GeoXrefs with certainty qualifiers, constituent GeoXrefs (artist nationality/origin), style and period attributes, and attribution uncertainty prefixes ("possibly by X"). Dahlia demonstrated eMuseum's filtering capabilities and the current limitations of geography display: the system does not nest geography hierarchically, sometimes defaults to Getty TGN local-language names rather than the museum's preferred internal terms, and objects with uncertain geography (e.g., "France or China") can appear confusingly under both without any clear context for visitors. Madeline articulated a key strategic question: what percentage of the experience are we building for researchers versus the general public, and does the answer change how granular publicly-facing geography and metadata should be? Brita covered rights display, advocating for rightstatements.org icons on object records and the image request workflow, including a link on the object page pre-filled with accession number. On photography, the group confirmed that \~70% of the collection has photography at sufficient resolution, multi-angle shots are already being taken, and the main bottleneck is curator selection and open access clearance.

**Key Themes**

* Significant TMS data richness is not reflected on the public website \- closing this gap is a foundational pipeline priority  
* Text formatting (paragraph breaks) must be fixed before new fields are added \- this is a critical urgency and P1 priority for phase 1\.  
* Geography display needs hierarchical nesting, internal-term defaults (not Getty TGN defaults), and certainty qualifiers  
* Rights statement icons (rightstatements.org) should appear on object records with clickable explanations  
* The image request workflow needs a streamlined, pre-filled form linked from the object page  
* Photography coverage is better than perceived, the bottleneck is curator selection and open access clearance  
* Style, period, and subject attributes are a high-value future filter but require a data entry program before they can be surfaced

**Key Quotes**  
"Formatting outranks new filter options. If we're already doing our hierarchy of needs, formatting comes first." — Madeleine DiBiasi

"Achenbach can't search by artist nationality on eMuseum. If we can do that on the website, I think they'll abandon eMuseum pretty quickly." — Kathleen Forrest

"Any attribute that's populated should show up in some way, unless we mark it as internal — having as much of the curators' contextual cataloging available makes all the difference for external research." — Dahlia Terrio

## Curatorial Fellows {#curatorial-fellows}

**Participants:** Tobi (Curatorial Cataloging Fellow, European Paintings), Camilla (Curatorial Cataloging Fellow, Achenbach), Felix (Curatorial Cataloging Fellow, Ancient Art), Tali (Curatorial Cataloging Fellow, European Decorative Arts \+ Sculpture)

The Curatorial Fellows session brought a cross-departmental, data-forward perspective as they spend the most time cataloging records that will eventually reach the public. Felix flagged a specific scholarly need: alternate/legacy accession numbers should be searchable, as that is how certain objects are cited in seminal academic literature. * A clean, layered object record with rich art historical content and great images is the north star — not complexity
