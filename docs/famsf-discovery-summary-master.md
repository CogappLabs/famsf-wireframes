# 2026 Collections Project 

## Master FAMSF Discovery Summary

# Project context

This summarizes findings from six discovery activities:

* Stakeholder interviews across ten FAMSF departments (March–April 2026\)  
* Hotjar web survey run April–May 2026  
* Gap analysis drawing on FGW audience research (2024) and Web 2.0 discovery (2025)  
* Landscape audit of 10 peer institutions  
* UX audit of the current FAMSF collections experience  
* Content audit of the current FAMSF collections experience  
   

Together these provide a clear direction for the 2026 Collections project.

# Strategic Learnings \+ Opportunities

## **Strategic learnings**

*What the discovery process revealed, a synthesis of stakeholder interviews, Hotjar survey, gap analysis, landscape research, and content audit.*

1. **The gap between what FAMSF holds and what the public can access is the defining problem.** TMS contains significantly richer data than the public website reflects. Fields for multiple titles, subclassifications, GeoXrefs, constituent nationality, uncertainty qualifiers, style and period attributes all exist in the database but are not surfaced publicly. \~70% of the collection has photography at sufficient resolution for deep zoom and multi-angle photography is already being captured and with the ongoing Open Access work, \~7,000 works will be available to push to the web by the end of 2026\.  
     
2. **The current collection experience obscures institutional stature.** Multiple stakeholder groups independently noted that the true depth and breadth of the collection is invisible online. The Achenbach holds one of the largest works-on-paper collections in the US, the Rodin collection and French art holdings are globally significant, and Alma Spreckels' collection is well linked to FAMSF, however none of this is legible on the current site. Peer institutions like AIC, NGA, and the Rijksmuseum treat the online collection as a primary vehicle for institutional identity but FAMSF does not.  
     
3. **Search failure has a compounding effect across all audiences.** Search isn't just broken for researchers, its current limitations affect curatorial workflows, educator planning, donor stewardship, and public perception of the institution. Despite high completion rates by researchers across various tasks, there are significant failures being experienced such as discrepancy between eMuseum and the public site results for the same search, missing filters, full-page reloads, Getty TGN naming inconsistencies, attribution qualifiers absent from thumbnails, a zero-results dead end with no recovery path, and disorganized filter ordering with non-searchable filter fields.   
     
4. **The object detail page is critical but currently underbuilt.** Research from the FGW audience study, stakeholder interviews, and ongoing industry research identify direct Google search to an object page as a common first point of contact for visitors. Current object detail pages lack critical image features, linked editorial and educational content, clickable metadata, and a forward path for discovery. Many pages are also missing or contain outdated introductory descriptions and fields like provenance, bibliography, and exhibition history are not consistently populated.  
     
5. **Content organization impedes discovery.** The current website organizes artworks by the curatorial department in ways that reflect how internal team members think about art. That said, named sub-collections such as the Anderson Graphic Arts Collection are invisible without a direct link. Topic pages are largely static, limited to two active themes, and cannot be expanded or edited without development work. “Keep exploring” recirculation module is also static, significantly limiting its effectiveness as a discovery aid. The collection landing page fails to communicate collection character, institutional identity and to serve different audiences by providing meaningful entry points for each.   
     
6. **Collection site failures lead to additional staff operational burdens.** Curators often bypass the public site in favor of eMuseum or Google to check specific artwork details, prepare for upcoming exhibitions, or download artwork for presentation purposes. Additionally, team members are often forced to manually assist external researchers who are unable to navigate the current collections experience. The collections website's failures result in ongoing operational burdens on staff that a functional platform would eliminate or significantly reduce.  
     
7. **AI for infrastructure and to enhance curatorial voices, not replace them.** There is openness to AI with the caveat of it being almost exclusively for backend applications: auto-tagging, constituent linking, bibliographic deduplication, image QA. Front-facing AI features are met with real concern around Eurocentric bias, hallucination, citation integrity, and the accountability gap for curatorial staff. The exception is a verified, publication-fed tool that draws from confirmed FAMSF content (ex: the Education team's reading-level adaptation concept).   
     
8. **FAMSF fails to meet industry standards across the collections experience.** Leading peer institutions such as AIC, NGA, Cleveland, and the Rijksmuseum have invested in online collections as primary digital products with calibrated multi-audience entry points, robust object pages, and interactive discovery tools. FAMSF's current experience falls short on capabilities that are now considered baseline (deep zoom, linked metadata tags, dynamic filtering, contextual recommendations).   
     
9. **Collection related content exists that isn't being used.** Substantial institutional content (publications, labels, essays, audio tours, and articles) has already been produced but is not linked to or surfaced from collection records. The only current cross-linking is a limited number of audio items on object detail pages and stories on topic pages.

## **Strategic opportunities**

*Where the Collections 2026 project can make the highest-impact as informed by discovery.*

1. **Improve the underlying data pipeline to create a scalable foundation.** Automating the TMS-to-website pipeline and surfacing the data richness that already exists in the database is the single highest-leverage investment. This unblocks advanced search, enriches object pages, enables meaningful filtering, and reduces curatorial workarounds.  
     
2. **Position images as a primary part of the collection experience.** Institutions such as the Rijksmuseum invest in an artwork-led collections experience, creating visually engaging pages that complement robust metadata. IIIF implementation with deep zoom, multi-view galleries, open access downloads, and a clear rights communication framework addresses the single most universally requested improvement across every research stream. With \~70% of the collection already at qualifying resolution meaning that the investment would immediately be felt with the phase 1 launch.  
     
3. **Redesign collection organization and landing pages as multi-audience entry points.** Move away from organizing solely by the curatorial department toward multi-dimensional discovery pathways. Make departmental and thematic categorizations reflect how different audiences think about art. Surface named sub-collections (e.g., Anderson Graphic Arts Collection) as findable, linkable destinations. Make topic/theme pages dynamic and editable without development effort, and expand them. Redesign the collections landing page to serve distinct audiences: search-first entry for researchers, curated thematic and mood-based entry points for browsers and educators, and institutional framing that foregrounds FAMSF's distinctive strengths. Add "most viewed" and recently added modules to increase organic discovery.  
     
4. **Redesign the object detail page as a critical part of the discovery pathway.** Incorporate rich image features, expanded metadata, clickable tags that launch filtered searches, linked editorial content, rights communication, and multiple types of recommendations modules to change the object detail pages from dead ends to powerful circulation mechanism on the site. Add formatted citations for easy researcher use, surface gallery location with subject name (matched to Bloomberg descriptors), and standardize introductory web text using label copy as a potential default. Structure the page to surface visit-planner and browser needs first then follow up with deeper researcher and educator content below.  
     
5. **Leverage the object detail page as a conversion surface for visits and shop sales.** The object detail page is a critical part of the collection visitor journey and presents an opportunity for introducing key conversion activities to a traditionally non-conversion related area of the website. For works that are on view, a "Get tickets" CTA bridges the gap between casual browsing and active visit-planning. For works with related merchandise in the shop (prints, catalogues, books, etc), surfacing relevant merchandise creates a natural path to purchase. For visit-planners (\~67% of all famsf.org users) and art-curious browsers (\~37% of collection survey respondents), these two potential implementations offer high conversion opportunities.  
     
6. **Make content connectivity a design principle, not an afterthought.** Establish bidirectional links between object pages and editorial content (articles, essays, audio tours, past exhibitions) to increase value without requiring new content production. Replace static "Keep Exploring" recirculation with dynamic modules.  
     
7. **Build a search experience that reflects the actual collection.** Rebuild the search experience with dynamic filtering, no full-page reloads, filter hierarchy, and expanded fields (such as geography, culture as distinct filters, donor/collector, material/technique, attribution qualifiers, accession number) to close the gap between eMuseum and the public site. Introduce auto-suggestions, exploration prompts, and improved zero-results experience to improve experience for all audiences. Improve filter usability with logical ordering, searchable filter fields, and resolved duplicate values. Adding features such as expanded filter types (periods and styles, highlights, open access, popular, credit line, object number), custom date-range control, and multiple results-sorting options (relevance, title A–Z, date, artist/maker, accession number) could also help to improve the experience.  
     
8. **Deploy AI where it amplifies curatorial voice, with clear guardrails.** Rather than treating AI as a single decision, the opportunity is to pursue it selectively where the case is strongest and the risks are lowest: AI-enhanced search, backend tagging and data enrichment (auto-tagging, constituent linking, bibliographic deduplication, image QA). Any front-facing AI features should be clearly labeled, opt-in/out for educator contexts, and approved against an explicit sourcing and accountability framework.  
     
9. **Use the collections project as an opportunity for donor stewardship and identity investment.** The Anderson family's inability to find their gift on the website is a concrete example of how the current website fails to strengthen and support existing donor relationships. More broadly, elevating collector and founder stories (Alma Spreckels, MH de Young, and others) tied to San Francisco's history is an opportunity to connect the institution to the city in a way the current site entirely fails to do. Provenance statements, donor attribution, and credit line searchability are currently seen across institutional peers.

# Phase 2: North Star vision

The 2026 Collections project begins with addressing critical technical debt and infrastructure issues and closing the gap between what FAMSF holds and what the public can access. The longer-term vision goes further by envisioning an online collection experience that functions as its own primary cultural destination and digital product.

* A research destination of industry leading / international standing → institutional reputation and scholarly standing  
* A discovery experience that supports all audiences → access and equity  
* A product that reflects FAMSF’s civic identify  
* Operational asset → operational sustainability / scalability
